3.1 Problem Setup and Overview

We study imitation learning for contact-rich manipulation, where force feedback reveals visually ambiguous constraints but varies in usefulness and may introduce spurious correlations under limited demonstrations. We therefore jointly learn action prediction and when to use force-aware control.

At time \(t\), let \(o_t\) contain camera observations, proprioception, and task context, and let \(w_t\in\mathbb{R}^{F}\) contain force, torque, and optional contact-force measurements. Their recent history is \(W_t=[w_{t-L+1},\ldots,w_t]\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times F}\). The policy \(\pi_\theta(A_t\mid o_t,W_t)\) predicts an \(H\)-step chunk \(A_t\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times(d_c+d_g)}\), with continuous-motion and gripper dimensions \(d_c\) and \(d_g\).

SPECTRA combines a force-blind coarse policy \(\pi_c(A_t\mid o_t)\), restricted to low-frequency DCT coefficients, with a force-aware fine policy \(\pi_f(A_t\mid o_t,W_t)\) that models the full spectrum. Both preserve full temporal resolution for gripper commands. As shown in Figure 2, we train the policies independently on \(\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{fit}}\), freeze them, and compare their prediction errors on held-out calibration data \(\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}}\). These comparisons supervise a gate \(g_\phi(W_t)\in[0,1]\) that selects one policy per new action chunk, jointly determining force conditioning and spectral support.

3.2 Spectral Action Representation

To control the motion detail available to the coarse and fine policies, we use the discrete cosine transform (DCT) to express each action sequence as a weighted sum of cosine curves. Slowly varying curves capture the overall motion, while faster-varying curves capture sharper changes. Let \(X_t\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times d_c}\) contain the normalized continuous actions and \(G_t\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times d_g}\) contain the normalized gripper commands. The orthonormal DCT-II computes the frequency coefficients as

\[C_t[n]=\gamma_n\sum_{j=0}^{H-1}X_t[j]\cos\left(\frac{\pi n(j+\frac{1}{2})}{H}\right).\tag{1}\]

Here \(X_t[j]\) is the continuous-action vector at step \(j\) within the chunk, and \(C_t[n]\) contains its coefficients at frequency index \(n\in\{0,\ldots,H-1\}\). The normalization factors are \(\gamma_0=1/\sqrt{H}\) and \(\gamma_n=\sqrt{2/H}\) for \(n>0\). The coarse policy keeps only the lowest-frequency coefficients, producing smooth action chunks, while the fine policy can use all coefficients to capture faster changes. Gripper commands remain in the time domain, preserving a prediction at every step.

Inspired by EqualSNR Falck et al. (2025), we rescale the frequency coefficients so that diffusion noise affects them more evenly. Some coefficients vary much less than others across demonstrations, so the same amount of noise can hide their information sooner. We therefore put the coefficients on a common scale before adding noise:

\[Z_t=C_t/\hat\sigma,\tag{2}\]

where division is element-wise and \(\hat\sigma\) contains the standard deviation of each frequency coefficient in each action dimension. Each policy estimates these values from its first training batch and keeps them fixed. We set a minimum value of \(10^{-6}\) to avoid division by zero. This scaling gives the coefficients approximately equal variance, making the added noise comparable relative to their variation. We then train the diffusion model on \(Y_t=[Z_t,G_t]\), which combines the rescaled motion coefficients with the normalized gripper commands. After sampling, we multiply the predicted coefficients by \(\hat\sigma\) to restore their original scales, then apply the inverse DCT to recover the continuous actions.

3.3 Coarse and Fine Diffusion Policies

Using the representation \(Y_t\), we train the coarse and fine policies independently on \(\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{fit}}\). Both receive the current observations \(o_t\)—camera images, proprioception, and task context—while the fine policy additionally receives the force/tactile memory \(W_t\). At a sampled diffusion step \(s\), we construct the noisy input \(Y_t^{(s)}=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_s}\,Y_t+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_s}\,\epsilon\), where \(\bar\alpha_s\) controls the signal–noise balance and \(\epsilon\) is independent standard Gaussian noise. The policies predict this noise as

\[\hat\epsilon_c=\epsilon_{\theta_c}(Y_t^{(s)},s,o_t),\qquad\hat\epsilon_f=\epsilon_{\theta_f}(Y_t^{(s)},s,o_t,W_t).\tag{3}\]

with respective parameters \(\theta_c\) and \(\theta_f\). Each predictor comprises an observation encoder and a conditional one-dimensional U-Net. The coarse policy uses the observation embedding directly; the fine policy augments it with information retrieved from \(W_t\) through cross-attention before conditioning the U-Net.

We control motion detail through a binary loss mask \(M_m\), shaped like \(Y_t\), for each policy \(m\in\{c,f\}\). The coarse mask retains only the lowest \(k\) frequencies of each continuous-action dimension and all gripper entries; the fine mask retains every entry. Both policies minimize

\[\mathcal{L}_m=\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{mean}\left(M_m\odot(\hat\epsilon_m-\epsilon)^2\right)\right],\tag{4}\]

where \(\odot\) denotes element-wise multiplication, the mean covers the full action tensor, and the expectation averages over demonstrations, diffusion steps, and noise. Both policies generate complete action chunks. Before decoding, we zero the coarse policy’s excluded coefficients, restricting its continuous predictions to low frequencies, while retaining the fine policy’s full spectrum. Further details are provided in Appendix D.

3.4 Learning the Force Gate

After training, we freeze both policies and use their relative prediction errors to supervise the gate. For each calibration example in \(\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}}\), we sample a chunk \(\hat A_{t,m}\) from each policy \(m\in\{c,f\}\) and compare it with the demonstrated chunk \(A_t\) in the same action coordinates and scale:

\[e_{t,m}=\frac{\|\hat A_{t,m}-A_t\|_F^2}{H(d_c+d_g)},\qquad y_t=\mathbf{1}[\rho e_{t,f}<e_{t,c}].\tag{5}\]

Here \(e_{t,m}\) is the mean squared prediction error, and \(y_t=1\) favors the fine policy. The parameter \(\rho>0\) controls the selection margin; with \(\rho>1\), the fine policy must improve prediction accuracy sufficiently to be preferred.

The gate maps the flattened force/tactile memory \(W_t\) to \(p_t=g_\phi(W_t)\in[0,1]\) using a small multilayer perceptron with a sigmoid output, where \(\phi\) denotes its parameters. We train it with binary cross-entropy:

\[\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{gate}}=-\mathbb{E}[y_t\log p_t+(1-y_t)\log(1-p_t)].\tag{6}\]

We split the calibration examples into gate-training and gate-validation subsets, using the former for the expectation above and the latter for checkpoint selection. Both action policies remain frozen. At deployment, the gate requires only \(W_t\) to predict which policy to select.

3.5 Gated Action Generation

At each replanning step, the gate computes \(p_t=g_\phi(W_t)\) and selects the policy that generates the next action chunk:

\[\hat A_t\sim\begin{cases}\pi_f(\cdot\mid o_t,W_t),&p_t>\tau,\\\pi_c(\cdot\mid o_t),&p_t\le\tau,\end{cases}\tag{7}\]

where \(\tau\in[0,1]\) is the gate threshold. The selected policy generates the entire chunk, including gripper commands. Its continuous-action coefficients are rescaled and decoded through the inverse DCT. The controller then executes a prefix of the decoded chunk before replanning, while updating the force/tactile memory at every control step. This allows policy selection to change as the interaction unfolds.
